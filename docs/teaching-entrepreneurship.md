# What I know about teaching entrepreneurship

I learned Babson College's method of teaching entrepreneurship, brought it into a Costa Rican school I had graduated from, and then helped carry it into the public rural high schools. This page is what I took from that work, and why it shapes the vault in this repo.

## What I did

- **Lincoln School, Costa Rica (2018–19).** As a consultant, I ran Babson's Entrepreneurial Thought & Action workshops for teachers, staff and students, and worked with Babson's Lewis Institute on how to take the method from one private school to public schools.
- **Yo Emprendedor (from 2019).** As director, I worked with an alliance that trained teachers in the Ministry of Public Education's (MEP) rural high schools.
- **The MEP guides (2021).** I was part of the external commission (Asociación Yo Emprendedor) for the Ministry's official teaching guides for the socio-productive area of the *liceos rurales*, grades 7 to 10 (ISBN 978-9977-60-422-0 to -425-1). These schools serve "más de once mil estudiantes" (more than eleven thousand students) in more than 130 rural high schools. The alliance behind the guides: the Alianza Empresarial para el Desarrollo and the MEP, with Yo Emprendedor, EY, BAC Credomatic and Fundación Omar Dengo.

The guides train teachers in five practices, and the 8th-grade guide uses the European Commission's EntreComp framework. The rest of this page is those two ideas and what I learned applying them.

**The case I point to:** in 2019, at Liceo Rural El Progreso, near the Panama border, ten 16-year-olds put in ₡1,000 each and, following the guide, turned ₡10,000 into ₡380,000 in nine months. → [The caldosa de montaña case](case-caldosa-de-montana.md)

## The method: five practices

Babson teaches entrepreneurship as a **method**, not a process: "a way of thinking and acting built on a set of assumptions using a portfolio of practices to encourage creating", which "requires continuous practice" (Neck, Greene and Brush, 2014, pp. 10–13). A process assumes known inputs and outputs, like an assembly line. Entrepreneurship doesn't have those.

| Practice | The authors (2014, pp. 16–17; Vol. 2, 2021) | The guide I worked on (MEP, 2021) |
|---|---|---|
| **Play** | "the development of a free and imaginative mind, allowing one to see a wealth of possibilities, a world of opportunities" | "permitir que cultivemos una mente libre e imaginativa para que se abran todas las posibilidades" — let us grow a free, imaginative mind so every possibility opens up |
| **Empathy** | "a social and emotional skill that helps us feel and understand the emotions, circumstances, intentions, thoughts, and needs of others" (after McLaren) | "entender qué se siente 'caminar en sus zapatos'… qué valoran y, en última instancia, qué necesitan" — understand what it feels like to walk in their shoes… what they value and, in the end, what they need |
| **Creation** | "unleashing the creative ability of students to produce something of value with what they have" | "Crear y desarrollar soluciones para satisfacer esas necesidades" — create and develop solutions that meet those needs |
| **Experimentation** | "trying something, seeing what the results are, learning from the results, and then trying it again" | "Las ideas por sí solas no tienen valor, es la ejecución de una idea lo que la convierte en realidad" — ideas alone have no value; executing an idea is what makes it real |
| **Reflection** | "encourages students to codify their learning experiences… perhaps the most integrating of all the practices" | "la reflexión es una acción, que si constantemente estamos practicando nos permite aprender a aprender" — reflection is an action that, practiced constantly, lets us learn how to learn |

Examples from the book's 42 exercises: *Marshmallow tower* and the *Business model canvas game* (play), *The power of observation* and *Creating customer personas* (empathy), *The resource challenge* (creation), *Escalating market tests* and *$5, $50, and $500 experiments* (experimentation), *Opportunity walk* and *Developing a reflective practice* (reflection).

**Why a method beats content.** The authors: "information is a commodity today; therefore, 'we need to teach methods that stand the test of dramatic changes in content and context'" (2014, p. 11, quoting Neck and Greene, 2011). The guide says it in Spanish: because the goal is a set of skills, "se torna mucho más importante el cómo se enseña que el contenido propiamente" — how it is taught matters much more than the content itself.

## The map: EntreComp

The five practices say *how* students learn. EntreComp names *what* grows: 15 competences in 3 areas, across 8 levels from "Discover" to "Transform" (Joint Research Centre, 2016). Full tables and how this vault uses them: [entrecomp.md](entrecomp.md).

## What I learned in classrooms

These come from my working notes on teaching entrepreneurship.

**Mindset before tools.** Teaching business plans prepares a student to start one company. Teaching the mindset prepares them to be entrepreneurial in anything.

**Seven competencies teachers can actually use.** EntreComp's fifteen are the right map and too many for a teacher on a Monday morning. I worked with seven, each traceable to EntreComp:

| Competency | EntreComp |
|---|---|
| Opportunity recognition | 1.1 Spotting opportunities |
| Bias toward action | 3.1 Taking the initiative |
| Resourcefulness | 2.3 Mobilizing resources |
| Comfort with failure and iteration | 3.3 Coping with uncertainty, ambiguity and risk · 3.5 Learning through experience |
| Empathy and customer centricity | 1.4 Valuing ideas |
| Communication and storytelling | 2.5 Mobilizing others |
| Data literacy and decision making | 2.4 Financial and economic literacy · 3.3 |

**Same philosophy, different execution.** A rural school and an urban private school need different projects, timelines and tools, not a different method. Rural: start from the community (map its resources and problems, interview neighbors), a notebook is enough, a minimum viable product can be ten handmade units. Urban: more technology and market sophistication, more distraction and competition.

**The teacher becomes a facilitator.**

| Traditional | Entrepreneurial |
|---|---|
| I have the answers | Let's find out together |
| Follow the instructions | Experiment and learn |
| There is one right way | There are many paths |
| Don't fail | Fail fast, learn fast |
| I grade the result | Reality grades the result |

**Assess the process, not only the outcome.** A project can fail and the learning succeed. The weighting I proposed: 40% process and effort (documentation, weekly reflections, iteration on feedback), 30% demonstrated learning, 20% real impact (did they launch, get real feedback, create value for someone), 10% collaboration.

**It's a method, not another subject.** The argument for principals: "No es materia nueva, es método nuevo para enseñar lo que ya deben enseñar" — it's not a new subject; it's a new method to teach what schools already have to teach.

**What we really teach.** Courage to try, resilience to get up after failing, creativity to solve problems with little, empathy to understand others, and agency: the belief that "I *can* change my reality".

## What this means for an expertise app

| From the classroom | In the vault |
|---|---|
| How it's taught matters more than the content | The app structures practice, not just storage: five practice folders, one agent skill each |
| Reflection is learning how to learn | Reflection is weekly, in the student's words; the agent asks and never answers |
| The teacher is a facilitator | The agent is a facilitator: guess first, never pick the student's position |
| Reality grades the result | Experiments and creations bring outside feedback; results become sources |
| Assess the process | The dashboard shows the climb and the practice balance, never a score |
| Context changes execution | The same practices work with a phone and a notebook or with a full AI stack |
